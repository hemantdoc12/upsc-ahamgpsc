Post-flood water recovery · Five questions, fifteen connected angles

The flood has gone. Is the water safe now?

Follow a hypothetical post-flood water recovery: suitable tankers, staged network repairs, waste disposal and complete-cycle delivery planning.

All cases and figures are hypothetical. WHO technical guidance is not an Indian legal rule. This lesson is exam preparation, not an operational emergency manual. Follow competent local authorities for real safety decisions. No claim of a real flood or outbreak.

Fifteen connections

01 / 15

Separate the end of flooding from the end of exposure

In a hypothetical town, floodwater has receded and roads are passable. That observation describes surface conditions, not the safety of drinking water. WHO explains that floods can carry faecal material from the ground, overflowing latrines and sewers into wells, boreholes and surface sources. Damage to treatment works and distribution pipes can create other routes. The useful question is not simply whether water remains in the street, but which contamination pathways are still open between source and user. An increased risk is not proof that every source is contaminated or that an outbreak has occurred.

Use the week's water chain as a recovery map

Connect the week's lessons without treating every intervention as interchangeable. Runoff can move contamination; groundwater is not automatically protected from flood entry; treatment must address the relevant hazard; sanitation prevents fresh faecal exposure; and schools need functioning services, not just repaired walls. A recovery map can follow source, transport, storage and use, with sanitation and hygiene beside that chain. This map is a teaching synthesis of WHO's emergency priorities and UNICEF's service approach. It helps a Mains answer move from a physical pathway to a specific protective action, rather than listing unrelated schemes.

03 / 15

Map damage before treating the network as one unit

WHO's small-network rehabilitation note starts with assessing damage. Existing drawings, local operators' knowledge and inspection help locate damaged valves, broken pipes and missing sections. An initial emergency survey focuses on visible major damage so basic supply can be restored; hidden or smaller breaks may still need later work. This is a staged response, not a claim that the first inspection finds every defect. In the hypothetical town, a map linking break locations to supply branches is more useful for restoration than a single town-wide label saying that the network is damaged.

network

04 / 15

Isolate a damaged branch, not every unaffected user

The WHO note recommends isolating affected sections from the rest of the distribution network, usually through control valves. Isolation reduces water wastage and allows supply to continue to unaffected areas. Repair can then proceed in planned sections, beginning near the source and moving outward. Whether an actual network can be operated this way depends on its layout and functioning controls. The conceptual distinction is between stopping flow to a damaged branch and unnecessarily stopping all supply. Isolation does not itself clean the damaged pipe or certify the water's safety.

network

05 / 15

Provide an alternative while repair takes time

Where major network damage will take more than a few hours to repair, WHO's note calls for an alternative supply. Options include bottled drinking water or tankers serving people directly or filling temporary storage. The alternative must itself be suitable: availability of a vehicle or nearby source is only the beginning of the check. Keep users informed about affected areas, the temporary system and what they should do. Emergency delivery and network repair run together; one should not be mistaken for proof that the other has been completed.

06 / 15

Choose a tanker by its history, not its paint

WHO's tank-cleaning note screens tanks by previous contents, ease of cleaning and storage hygiene. It says selected tanks should only have held food-grade liquids; tanks previously used for fuel or sewage should not be used. For example, in our hypothetical case, repainting a former fuel tanker does not make it a drinking-water tanker merely because it is available or has been rinsed. Prior food use is not an automatic clearance either. A suitable milk tanker, for example, still needs the required cleaning and disinfection before carrying drinking water. Suitability comes before treatment.

07 / 15

Cleaning and disinfection are different tasks

WHO sets out selection, cleaning, disinfection and safe waste disposal as separate stages. Cleaning removes residual contents and deposits from internal surfaces, including corners and joints; washing and flushing remove the cleaning agent. Disinfection follows cleaning. A record that disinfectant was added cannot stand in for evidence that physical cleaning was done. Equally, a visibly clean surface is not by itself evidence that the disinfection stage was completed. This distinction is useful far beyond tankers: removing material and reducing microbial contamination are related purposes, not identical operations.

08 / 15

Include the hose and pump in the supply chain

A clean tank can still be paired with dirty filling or discharge equipment. WHO's note includes cleaning the hoses, pumps and pipes used to fill and empty it, followed by the relevant disinfection steps. Its tanker-delivery note also calls for hoses and couplings to be stored in a sealed container to protect them from contamination. Thus an audit should trace the water's contact surfaces rather than inspect only the largest object. A photograph of a clean tank cannot show how a hose was cleaned, stored or used.

09 / 15

Do not send the cleaning waste into the next water body

Cleaning, disinfection and rinsing produce liquid waste that needs planned disposal. WHO warns against disposing of this waste into rivers and ponds because organic material and high chlorine concentrations may harm fish and plants. Sudden discharge can also cause local erosion or flooding. Its listed disposal routes include a sewer network or transport to a sewage treatment plant. The appropriate route must be chosen for the site with competent operators. Protecting drinking water cannot mean shifting the cleaning waste into another water body. Tank entry and chemical handling are hazardous work, not do-it-yourself classroom instructions.

tank

10 / 15

Calculate delivered water, not parked capacity

WHO's tanker-planning method calculates capacity from complete delivery cycles: filling, outward travel, offloading and return. It also allows for delays and maintenance in its worked example. A fleet's tank volume multiplied by its vehicle count is only one load, not its daily output. In a simplified hypothetical calculation, a 4,000-litre tanker completing four full loads delivers 16,000 litres. Real planning must separately allow for access, road condition, fuel, drivers, maintenance, source availability and safe receiving storage. Arithmetic estimates quantity; it does not certify water quality. In a fixed shift that counts only complete return cycles, round the cycle count down. Deduct any stated preparation or maintenance time before counting that vehicle's cycles; do not treat output as continuously divisible.

tanker

11 / 15

Verify quality along delivery, not only at filling

WHO's delivery note asks planners to check that the filling source has sufficient quantity and acceptable water quality. It also calls for chlorine testing equipment, trained use and checks before water is discharged. These are checks at different points, not competing alternatives. Source quality does not remove the need for suitable equipment and controlled handling during transport. A delivery record should therefore link where and when water was collected to where, when and how much was delivered, with the relevant quality checks. No single residual reading proves the absence of every possible chemical hazard.

tanker · cdc

12 / 15

Match treatment to the hazard

Flood response must distinguish microbial hazards from fuel or toxic chemicals. India's National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) recommends boiling or disinfection as appropriate for microbial risks, while its flood advisory warns that these methods do not make water containing fuel or toxic chemicals safe. CDC gives the same chemical-hazard warning. Follow competent local authorities and use an appropriate alternative source where such contamination is known or suspected. This revisits the week's treatment boundary, not a new MCQ pattern. In recovery, a faster vehicle cannot compensate for an unsuitable source or tank history.

ncdc · cdc · tank

13 / 15

Keep sanitation, hygiene and vector control alongside supply

WHO identifies safe water, basic sanitation and good hygiene as linked emergency priorities. Restoring delivered water does not remove exposure from damaged sanitation or eliminate the need for handwashing. NCDC separately identifies stagnant water in discarded containers and other small collections as mosquito-breeding opportunities. Removing those collections addresses a vector pathway; it does not disinfect drinking water. Conversely, disinfecting drinking water does not remove all breeding sites. A good recovery plan assigns actions to the pathway each one interrupts rather than calling any one activity complete disease prevention.

emergency · ncdc

14 / 15

Restore a school's services, not only its buildings

UNICEF's 2025 framework treats climate-resilient school WASH as a service problem involving infrastructure, resources, service providers, users and governance. Recovery should consider safe and reliable water, sanitation and hygiene, including the needs of the most exposed and vulnerable users. A repaired roof or a new storage tank is not the entire service. Planning also needs maintenance, responsible operators, communication and a way to cope with the next disruption. This is a resilience framework, not an automatic school-reopening certificate or a new legal checklist for India.

15 / 15

Close recovery with evidence at each stage

For the hypothetical town, build a recovery table with five columns: remaining hazard, affected part of the chain, action, responsible operator and follow-up evidence. Pipe repair should be followed by the required testing, cleaning and disinfection; temporary supply needs suitable equipment, enough delivered water and quality checks; schools need usable services. WHO's tanker note recommends judging contracted service by the quantity and quality delivered rather than operating time alone. This table is a teaching synthesis, not a statutory form. The final test is whether each action addresses the risk it claims to control.

Five Prelims questions

Questions 1-2 form Prelims 1/3, 3-4 form Prelims 2/3, and question 5 is HARD. Choose first, then examine every option.

Question 1 / 5

A relief team applies WHO's tank-selection and preparation guidance. Consider:
1. Previous use for a food-grade liquid is enough to establish that a tank is ready to carry drinking water.
2. A tank formerly used for water but left idle still requires cleaning and disinfection.
3. A tank formerly used for fuel passes the selection stage once the same cleaning and disinfection process is completed.
4. Ease of cleaning and protected storage remain relevant even when the previous liquid was food-grade.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. Statement 2 is correct, but statement 1 confuses a prior-use selection criterion with completed preparation and overall suitability.

B. Correct. Statements 2 and 4 follow the guidance: idle former-water tanks still need cleaning and disinfection, while cleanability and protected storage are separate selection considerations.

C. Wrong. Statement 4 is correct; statements 1 and 3 are not. Food-grade history does not establish readiness, and cleaning/disinfection does not remove the exclusion of former fuel tanks.

D. Wrong. Statements 2 and 4 are correct, but statement 3 is not. The former-fuel restriction is a selection rule, not simply an instruction to carry out more preparation.

Question 2 / 5

A flood damages a small piped network. Its valves can separate damaged branches from unaffected areas. Applying WHO's rehabilitation guidance, consider:
1. Isolating affected branches can reduce wastage while allowing supply to unaffected areas.
2. An initial survey focused on visible major damage establishes that no hidden damage remains.
3. A full network survey may be impracticable while restoring basic emergency supply.
4. Users should be told which sections are affected and what they should do to protect their health and safety.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: D

A. Wrong. Statement 1 is correct, but statement 2 mistakes the initial focus on visible major damage for proof that all hidden damage has been ruled out.

B. Wrong. Statements 3 and 4 are correct, but statement 2 is not. The emergency survey's limited scope is not an assurance that no hidden damage remains.

C. Wrong. Statements 1 and 3 are correct, but this answer omits statement 4. Informing users about affected sections and protective actions is part of the guidance.

D. Correct. Statements 1, 3 and 4 describe compatible duties: isolate affected sections where possible, restore a basic supply even when a full survey is initially impracticable, and keep users informed. None of these establishes that the whole network is free of damage.

Question 3 / 5

Two emergency tanker contracts use different payment bases. P pays for vehicle operating time. Q pays for the quantity and quality of water delivered. Under WHO's tanker-management guidance, which assessment is justified?

Correct answer: C

A. Wrong. WHO recommends basing contract fees on quantity and quality delivered, not operating time. Ease of observing an input does not make it the recommended fee basis.

B. Wrong. The fee basis is appropriate, but WHO separately calls for clarifying responsibilities for fuel, insurance, maintenance and driver wages. An outcome-based fee does not settle those allocations.

C. Correct. Q matches the recommended fee basis. WHO separately says to agree on a method of appraising performance, so selecting that basis does not complete the contract design.

D. Wrong. Equal tank capacity does not make an operating-time fee equivalent to one based on quantity and quality delivered. The recommendation concerns the payment basis, not just vehicle size.

Question 4 / 5

Under WHO's guidance on waste from cleaning, disinfecting and rinsing water tanks, consider:
1. Reducing the chlorine concentration addresses the aquatic-life concern identified in the guidance; the remaining organic load need not affect disposal planning.
2. Organic material as well as high chlorine concentrations can make discharge into rivers and ponds harmful.
3. The guidance includes disposal to a sewer network or transport to a sewage treatment plant among its listed routes.
4. The disposal route need not account for local erosion or flooding if no chemical disinfectant was used.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: A

A. Correct. Statements 2 and 3 distinguish the waste's hazards from its disposal route. WHO names organic material and high chlorine levels, and lists sewer disposal or transport to a treatment plant among its options. It also lists a septic tank overflowing into an underground soakage system. The appropriate route depends on the site.

B. Wrong. Statement 3 is correct, but statement 1 treats chlorine as the only relevant aquatic-life concern. Organic material also matters in the guidance.

C. Wrong. Only statement 2 here is correct. Reducing chlorine does not remove the need to consider organic material, and sudden discharge can cause erosion or flooding independently of disinfectant use.

D. Wrong. Statements 2 and 3 are correct, but statement 4 confuses chemical hazard with the separate physical effects of releasing a large volume suddenly.

Question 5 / 5 · HARD

A hypothetical operation must deliver 44,000 litres in a 360-minute shift. Tanker A holds 6,000 litres and takes 100 minutes per complete delivery-and-return cycle; B holds 4,000 litres and takes 80 minutes. Both are prepared and available at shift start. Suitable tanker C holds 5,000 litres and takes 90 minutes per cycle, but first needs 90 minutes of preparation within the shift. Each load counts only if its complete cycle ends within the shift. Vehicles start empty; preparation cannot overlap a cycle. Ignore other delays and losses; source and receiving capacity are sufficient. Consider:
1. A and B together can initially deliver 34,000 litres, not their proportionally calculated fractional-cycle output.
2. After preparation, C can complete four cycles because its driving-and-delivery time fits four times into 360 minutes.
3. If A instead loses the first 70 minutes to maintenance, its shift delivery falls by 4,200 litres.
4. With that 70-minute loss for A and the stated preparation for C, total delivery is 43,000 litres, below the requirement.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. Statement 1 is correct: A completes 3 cycles and B 4, delivering 18,000 + 16,000 = 34,000 litres. Statement 2 forgets C's preparation time: only 270 minutes remain, allowing 3 cycles, or 15,000 litres.

B. Correct. Statement 1 uses whole completed cycles. For statement 4, A has 290 minutes after maintenance and completes only 2 cycles, delivering 12,000 litres. Add B's 16,000 and C's 15,000: 43,000 litres, a 1,000-litre shortfall. Without A's maintenance delay, the three would deliver 49,000 litres. These are hypothetical planning figures, not a guarantee of quality.

C. Wrong. Statement 2 ignores preparation time. Statement 3 incorrectly scales output continuously with time: A drops from 3 completed cycles to 2, so the loss is one 6,000-litre load, not 4,200 litres.

D. Wrong. Statement 4 is correct, but statement 3 is not. Because only complete cycles count, a 70-minute delay changes A's output by a whole load rather than by a proportional per-minute volume.
