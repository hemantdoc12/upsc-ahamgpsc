One news item, fifteen exam angles · 14 Nov 2026 · about eight minutes

The Rs 10 lakh tip: CCPA's dark-pattern order against Ola

Less to read. More to connect. The Central Consumer Protection Authority has fined Ola Rs 10 lakh for asking riders to pay extra - a 'tip', later an 'add-on' - while the app searched for a driver, promising a faster ride. The platform's own data said paying did not help.

The law caught up with the interface. The order calls the prompt an unfair trade practice and a dark pattern - 'confirm shaming', one of thirteen patterns India named in 2023. Rapido has already been fined; Uber and Namma Yatri are under investigation.

Fifteen connected angles

01 · News

The order

On 13 November 2026 the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) imposed a penalty of Rs 10 lakh on ANI Technologies Private Limited, which operates the ride-hailing platform Ola, and directed it to immediately discontinue its pre-ride 'tip'/'add-on' feature. The feature asked users for an extra payment while the app was searching for a driver, suggesting it could get them a ride faster. The Authority held the practice misleading, an unfair trade practice and a dark pattern.

02 · News

The company's own data convicted it

The strongest evidence in the order is Ola's own booking data for 1-15 June 2026. Ride requests carrying an add-on were accepted LESS often than those without: Delhi 38 per cent versus 62 per cent, Bengaluru 40 versus 68, Mumbai 39 versus 68. The app promised that paying extra would speed up a ride; the platform's own numbers showed the opposite, and consumers were never told. The CCPA held these omissions to be concealment of material information under Section 2(28)(iv) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

03 · Concept

What a dark pattern is

A dark pattern is a design choice in an interface meant to deceive, coerce or nudge users into decisions not in their interest. India's Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, issued by the CCPA on 30 November 2023 under Section 18 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, name 13 specified dark patterns, including false urgency, basket sneaking, drip pricing and confirm shaming. Ola's prompt, shown while a user waited for a driver, implied that not paying could mean a delayed or failed ride: the Authority held this fear-based nudge to be 'Confirm Shaming', Clause 3 of Annexure 1 to the Guidelines.

04 · Polity

The CCPA itself

The CCPA is a regulator created by the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (Chapter III). It is headed by a Chief Commissioner (currently Smt. Nidhi Khare, with Commissioner Shri Anupam Mishra on this order). It can inquire into violations suo motu, direct discontinuation of practices, order recalls and impose penalties. It is an executive regulator: it does not adjudicate individual disputes. That is the work of the District, State and National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, which are quasi-judicial.

05 · History

From the 1986 Act to the 2019 Act

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the three-decade-old 1986 Act. The old law's consumer forums were built for shop-floor grievances; the new one was built for platform markets. It created the CCPA, introduced product liability, brought e-commerce within the law's express reach and added mediation as a dispute route. The Ola order is the 2019 architecture working as designed: a regulator watching a whole class of consumers, not one complainant at a time.

06 · Concept

Unfair trade practice, defined

Section 2(47) of the 2019 Act defines an unfair trade practice to include unfair or deceptive methods and false or misleading representations about the benefits or usefulness of a service. Ola's prompts - 'Tip now to boost the chances of getting a ride sooner' - were representations about a benefit that the platform's own data did not support. That gap between the promise on the screen and the numbers behind it is what brought the feature inside Section 2(47).

07 · Concept

A tip is after, not before

A tip is ordinarily a voluntary payment made after a service is delivered. Soliciting it while the app searches for a driver converts a gratuity into a pre-service charge. The CCPA noted the payment was demanded on top of an upfront fare that already accounted for peak pricing and the driver's distance, so the consumer was being asked to pay a second premium for the same supply constraint. Renaming the 'tip' an 'add-on' changed nothing in substance - a labelling lesson that runs through consumer law.

08 · Economy

The aggregator rules were already clear

Clause 14.15 of the Motor Vehicles Aggregators Guidelines, 2025 provides for a tipping feature only after completion of the journey. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways clarified on 11 August 2026 that such prompts cannot be displayed before a ride is completed. Ola continued showing the prompt even after that clarification. The sequence matters: sectoral rule first, sectoral clarification second, consumer-regulator penalty third.

09 · Polity

Regulator versus commission

A common Prelims confusion pairs the CCPA with the consumer commissions. The CCPA is an executive regulator: it protects consumers as a class, investigates, and penalises. The Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions at district, state and national level are quasi-judicial bodies that hear individual complaints, and the National Commission hears appeals from State Commissions. Ola's penalty came from the first kind of body; an individual rider's refund claim would go to the second.

10 · Economy

Thirteen named dark patterns

The 2023 Guidelines do not ban 'dark patterns' in the abstract; they list thirteen specified ones. False urgency invents scarcity. Basket sneaking adds items at checkout without consent. Drip pricing hides the full price until the end. Confirm shaming uses guilt or fear as a lever. Forced action, subscription traps, interface interference, bait and switch, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick questions, SaaS billing and rogue malware complete the list. Naming them turns a vague sense of manipulation into enforceable categories.

11 · Economy

One order, whole industry

The CCPA has taken suo motu action against the practice across cab aggregators, not just Ola. It had earlier imposed a Rs 10 lakh penalty on Rapido (Roppen Transportation Services) for the same practice, and proceedings against Uber and Namma Yatri are under investigation. Class-wide enforcement is the point of a regulator: the cost of a design choice stops being a private calculation and becomes an industry-wide signal.

12 · Concept

Designed for the vulnerable moment

The order records that such payment requests appeared in bookings from hospitals, railway stations, inter-state bus terminals and airports. The Authority flagged who is on the other side of the screen in those places: patients, elderly persons, travellers carrying luggage, people travelling alone or at night. Consumer law calls this vulnerability; the interface met it at the exact moment of least bargaining power, with pre-set amounts anchoring users to higher figures and no option to close the prompt.

13 · Polity

Where a consumer actually complains

The release closes with the redress route: the National Consumer Helpline at 1915, or the CCPA directly by email. Individual monetary disputes go to the consumer commissions - and can be filed online through the e-Daakhil portal. Class-wide patterns go to the CCPA. Knowing which door to knock on is itself part of the 2019 Act's design.

14 · Economy

The behavioural economics underneath

The order reads like a catalogue of behavioural levers. Anchoring: pre-set amounts nudge users toward paying more. Default asymmetry: the platform alone knows how many drivers are free, so the user cannot judge whether paying helps. Loss framing: the prompt appears after booking, when abandoning the ride already feels like losing one. The guidelines and the order are behavioural economics translated into law - regulating the choice architecture, not just the price.

15 · Polity

The 2022 misleading-advertisement rules

Alongside the dark-patterns guidelines, the directions cite the Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022, notified on 9 June 2022. Those rules set the conditions for an advertisement to count as non-misleading and regulate bait and free-claim advertising. Together the 2022 and 2023 guidelines form the CCPA's standing toolkit: one governs what platforms say, the other how their screens make you choose.

Question 1

With reference to the November 2026 order of the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) against ANI Technologies Pvt Ltd (Ola), consider the following statements:

  1. The Authority imposed a penalty of Rs 10 lakh and directed discontinuation of the feature.
  2. The feature solicited an additional payment while the app was searching for a driver, before completion of the journey.
  3. The platform's own booking data showed that ride requests carrying the add-on were accepted more frequently than those without.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Correct answer: B

Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the CCPA imposed a Rs 10 lakh penalty and ordered the pre-ride 'tip'/'add-on' prompt discontinued. Statement 3 is incorrect: Ola's own data for 1-15 June 2026 showed requests with an add-on were accepted LESS often (Delhi 38% vs 62%, Bengaluru 40% vs 68%, Mumbai 39% vs 68%). Source: PIB, 13 Nov 2026 (PRID 2335741).

Source: PIB (Dept of Consumer Affairs), 13 Nov 2026, PRID 2335741 (Rs 10 lakh penalty; pre-ride tip/add-on; booking data 1-15 June 2026: Delhi 38% vs 62%, Bengaluru 40% vs 68%, Mumbai 39% vs 68%)

Question 2

With reference to the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), consider the following statements:

  1. It was established under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.
  2. It can inquire into violations of consumer rights suo motu and impose penalties.
  3. It is the apex quasi-judicial body that hears appeals from State Consumer Commissions.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

Correct answer: A

Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the CCPA is a 2019-Act regulator that can act suo motu and has just penalised Ola. Statement 3 is incorrect: the apex quasi-judicial consumer body that hears appeals from State Commissions is the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, not the CCPA.

Source: Consumer Protection Act, 2019 (CCPA, Chapter III); PIB PRID 2335741, 13 Nov 2026 (suo motu action; penalty); NCDRC hears appeals from State Commissions

Question 3

Under the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, the term 'Confirm Shaming' refers to:

Select the correct answer.

Correct answer: B

Option (b) is the definition of Confirm Shaming (Clause 3, Annexure 1); the CCPA held Ola's wait-time prompt created a sense of fear of not getting a ride. Option (a) is Basket Sneaking, (c) is a Subscription Trap, and (d) is False Urgency. The 2023 Guidelines were issued under Section 18 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

Source: Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, Clause 3 of Annexure 1 (Confirm Shaming); issued under Section 18, CPA 2019 on 30 Nov 2023 (PIB release 8 Dec 2023, PRID 1983994); PIB PRID 2335741

Question 4

Consider the following statements about tipping on ride-hailing platforms in India:

  1. Clause 14.15 of the Motor Vehicles Aggregators Guidelines, 2025 provides for a tipping feature only after completion of the journey.
  2. The Ministry of Road Transport and Highways clarified in August 2026 that such prompts could not be displayed before completion of the ride.
  3. The Motor Vehicles Aggregators Guidelines are issued by the Ministry of Heavy Industries.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

Correct answer: A

Statements 1 and 2 are correct: the 2025 aggregator guidelines allow tipping only post-journey, and MoRTH clarified on 11 August 2026 that pre-ride prompts cannot be displayed; Ola continued them regardless. Statement 3 is incorrect: the aggregator guidelines come from the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, not the Ministry of Heavy Industries.

Source: Motor Vehicles Aggregators Guidelines, 2025, Clause 14.15; MoRTH clarification of 11 Aug 2026 (both cited in PIB PRID 2335741)

Question 5

Which of the following pairs of specified dark patterns and their descriptions are correctly matched?

  1. Basket sneaking : addition of items such as donations at checkout without the user's consent
  2. Drip pricing : elements of the price not revealed upfront and disclosed only at the end of the buying process
  3. False urgency : falsely stating or implying scarcity or urgency to push an immediate action

Select the correct answer using the code given below.

Correct answer: D

All three pairs are correctly matched, as listed in the Annexure to the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023. The guidelines specify 13 dark patterns in all; confirm shaming - the one in the Ola order - is another.

Source: Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, Annexure (basket sneaking, drip pricing, false urgency definitions); PIB PRID 1983994, 30 Nov 2023
