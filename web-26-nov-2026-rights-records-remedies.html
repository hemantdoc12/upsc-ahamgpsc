Rights, records and remedies

One civic problem. Which right, record and remedy?

A fictional civic-service case separates rights, existing records and possible remedies.

01 / 15

A civic problem, several constitutional questions

Our fictional citizen faces an unresolved local service complaint and wants the relevant records. Separate the service request, information request, constitutional claim and possible remedy. A disappointing service does not automatically decide which right, duty, legal obligation or court process applies. The case teaches a method, not advice to file a particular proceeding or an allegation about a real authority.

02 / 15

Adoption and commencement are different milestones

Constitution Day on 26 November commemorates adoption on 26 November 1949. Article 394 provides that specified provisions came into force at once and the remaining provisions on 26 January 1950. Therefore both all provisions began on adoption and every provision first began on 26 January are inaccurate. The 2015 notification establishes the annual observance, not a universal bank or school holiday.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department · 19November2015 Constitution Day notification, reproduced in official MSME correspondence

03 / 15

Read who holds the right

Article 14 uses any person for equality before law/equal protection within India. Article 19(1) begins all citizens. Article 21 protects no person from deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. These differences matter. Do not treat all Fundamental Rights as citizen-only, or infer that citizenship is irrelevant to every right. We teach the selected text, not every judicial extension or limitation.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

04 / 15

Equality does not mean identical results in every case

Article 14 supplies equality before law and equal protection, not a promise that everyone must receive the same outcome irrespective of circumstances. A comparison requires facts and the applicable legal framework. The mere existence of different service outcomes does not itself prove unconstitutional discrimination, nor does an administrative explanation conclusively disprove it. Avoid deciding a real equality case from a slogan.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

05 / 15

Expression has a provision-specific restriction clause

Article 19(1)(a) concerns speech and expression; 19(2) lists grounds for reasonable restrictions by law. Those grounds include public order and defamation, among others. Disliking criticism is not itself a separately listed ground. The legal validity of a particular restriction needs more than naming a ground: the text requires its relevant conditions. This is a reading exercise, not a ruling on a real notice.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

06 / 15

Assembly is not a copy of the speech clause

Article 19(1)(b) protects assembly peaceably and without arms. Article 19(3) refers to reasonable restrictions by law in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India or public order. Do not copy every 19(2) speech ground into 19(3). Equally, calling an event an assembly does not remove the peaceable, unarmed or applicable restriction conditions.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

07 / 15

A service complaint is not a predetermined Article 21 judgment

Article 21 gives a powerful constitutional text, but a real claim still needs established facts and applicable interpretation. This lesson does not declare that every missed collection, delayed bill or unanswered representation is automatically an Article 21 violation. Identify the threatened interest and legal duty, then determine the evidence and remedy. An exam answer should preserve that conditional reasoning.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

08 / 15

Directive Principles are not disposable because they are non-justiciable

Article 37 says Part IV provisions are not enforceable by any court, while calling them fundamental in governance and placing a duty on the State to apply them in making laws. Article 47 addresses public health; 48A environmental protection. Non-enforceability under 37 does not mean these principles have no public-policy role. Nor does a policy objective alone create a guaranteed stand-alone court order.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

09 / 15

Rights, State principles and citizen duties have different addressees

Article 51A(g) makes environmental protection and compassion for living creatures a citizen duty. Article 48A directs the State to protect/improve the environment and safeguard forests/wildlife. Selected Fundamental Rights have their own wording and enforceability. A citizen duty does not, by itself, supply an executive officer with an unrestricted power to punish or cancel a right. A specific legal basis remains necessary.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

10 / 15

Article 32 has a defined constitutional purpose

Article 32 guarantees moving the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for enforcement of Part III rights. Its writ power is connected to those rights. That is not a constitutional promise that any unresolved administrative complaint, regardless of its legal basis, must receive relief under 32. A claimed violation and the appropriate proceeding still need assessment.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department

11 / 15

Article 226 is wider in purpose, not unlimited in operation

Article 226 covers Part III rights and any other purpose, and its second clause recognises a cause-of-action territorial link. That textual breadth differs from 32. It does not promise automatic admission, relief against any private dispute or freedom to ignore jurisdiction and other legal conditions. The official Gujarat High Court site identifies the institution, not the answer to a fictional writ petition.

Constitution of India, Legislative Department · High Court of Gujarat, official website

12 / 15

Ask for an existing record, not an invented answer

The Right to Information (RTI) definition includes material such as recorded opinions and advice. That differs from requiring a Public Information Officer (PIO) to create a new expert opinion or infer what no record says. Existing analysed data can also be a record; do not assume only raw numbers count. Our scoped sources support the distinction without claiming that every requested document must be disclosed irrespective of the Act.

Gujarat Information Commission, official RTI FAQ · ISTM official RTI case resource, definition of information

13 / 15

Information access and grievance correction are distinct

A person may request an existing inspection report or relevant file records from the appropriate public information officer, subject to the Act. Obtaining those records does not itself order the service repaired or award compensation. The information commission is not simply the initial counter for every municipal request. Follow the route relevant to information, service redress or constitutional relief rather than treating them as interchangeable.

Gujarat Information Commission, official RTI FAQ · ISTM official RTI case resource, definition of information

14 / 15

Match the question to the missing evidence

For our fictional service file, ask separately what rule or duty applies, what actually occurred, which existing record can establish it and what relief is sought. A record may help assess a rights claim without automatically proving that claim. Conversely, absence of a document in a supplied teaching file is not proof that no relevant record exists in the real world. Do not turn incomplete evidence into certainty.

15 / 15

A constitutional answer should state its limits

In Mains, identify the protected interest and addressee, read the specific provision, distinguish State policy from a citizen duty, then connect evidence and an appropriate conditional remedy. Use adoption/commencement dates accurately. Avoid promising a successful writ, treating RTI as an order to fix the service, or making every civic problem the same constitutional claim. Five Prelims questions

Questions 1-2 form Prelims 1/3; questions 3-4 form Prelims 2/3; question 5 alone is HARD. Choose first, then read the explanation for every option.

Question 1 / 5

With reference to constitutional adoption and commencement, consider:
1. Constitution Day commemorates adoption on 26 November 1949.
2. Every provision first came into force on 26 January 1950.
3. Article 394 provided immediate commencement for specified provisions.
4. The 2015 notification made 26 November a universal bank holiday.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: C

A. Wrong. Statement 1 is correct, but 2 ignores Article 394 immediate-commencement exceptions. The option also omits correct 3.

B. Wrong. Statement 3 correctly identifies the immediate-commencement exceptions, but statement 2 contradicts those exceptions. This option also omits correct statement 1.

C. Correct. The notification supports 1; Article 394 supports 3 and contradicts 2. Statement 4 is outside what the observance notification establishes.

D. Wrong. Statements 1 and 3 are correct, but adding 4 imports a holiday consequence not established by the notification.

Question 2 / 5

Using the express wording of the Constitution, consider:
1. Article 14 uses any person.
2. Article 19(1) begins all citizens.
3. Article 21 uses no person.
4. These provisions establish that every Fundamental Right is citizen-only.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. Statement 1 is correct, but 4 contradicts the person wording in 14 and 21. It also omits correct 2 and 3.

B. Correct. Statements 1-3 track the selected clauses; their different addressees defeat the universal citizen-only conclusion in 4.

C. Wrong. Statements 2 and 3 are correct, but Article 14 also expressly uses any person, making 1 correct.

D. Wrong. The first three textual statements are correct; 4 incorrectly universalises citizenship despite their differences.

Question 3 / 5

Concerning speech and peaceful assembly under Article 19, consider:
1. Article 19(1)(b) refers to assembly peaceably and without arms.
2. Public order appears in both 19(2) and 19(3).
3. Defamation is separately listed in 19(3) as a ground restricting assembly.
4. The restriction clauses 19(2) and 19(3) are textually identical.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: A

A. Correct. 1 matches the assembly right; 2 matches both restriction clauses. Defamation is listed in 19(2), not 19(3), and the clauses differ, defeating 3 and 4.

B. Wrong. 1 is correct, but 3 copies a speech-ground into the assembly clause; 2 is also omitted.

C. Wrong. 2 is correct, but 4 ignores their different enumerated grounds and scope. 1 is also omitted.

D. Wrong. 1 and 2 are correct, but adding 4 wrongly treats the clauses as identical.

Question 4 / 5

Which pairing best matches the constitutional text?

Correct answer: D

A. Wrong. 37 expressly calls the principles fundamental in governance and directs the State to apply them in making laws despite their non-enforceability by courts.

B. Wrong. 51A(g) states a citizen duty, not an unrestricted executive penalty power. A lawful basis and relevant conditions are separate.

C. Wrong. 32 concerns enforcement of Part III rights by appropriate proceedings, not every administrative dispute or guaranteed relief.

D. Correct. 226 has the broader stated purposes, with territorial/cause-of-action and other legal requirements still relevant. Breadth is not an assured outcome.

Question 5 / 5 · HARD

A fictional citizen seeks an existing municipal inspection report, asks a Public Information Officer (PIO) to invent a fresh technical opinion, and considers a writ for a statutory service duty without asserting a Part III right. Consider:
1. The existing report may be sought under Right to Information (RTI), subject to the Act.
2. RTI requires creation of the fresh opinion merely because it was requested.
3. Article 226 is textually wider in purpose than Article 32.
4. This description guarantees relief under Article 32.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: C

A. Wrong. 1 is qualified correctly; 2 confuses access to existing recorded opinions with a duty to create a fresh technical view. 3 is also correct and omitted.

B. Wrong. Statement 3 correctly distinguishes the purposes of Articles 226 and 32, but statement 2 wrongly requires a fresh unrecorded opinion. Correct statement 1 is omitted.

C. Correct. 1 preserves RTI conditions; 3 reflects 226 any-other-purpose wording versus 32 Part III enforcement. 2 and 4 overstate both information duty and remedy. This does not guarantee a 226 writ either.

D. Wrong. 1 and 3 are correct, but 4 guarantees 32 relief without an established Part III claim. A statutory service duty alone does not make that guarantee.
