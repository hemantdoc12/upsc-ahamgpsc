Supreme Court's special Sunday sitting on the Delhi protests, 11 Oct 2026

The interim directions are short. Behind them sit a colonial-era police power, a three-word phrase that took the Court 28 years to read properly, a protest case that rewrote the Constitution's text in 1951, and a Union Territory whose police do not answer to its own government. Each angle below is a doorway, not a question.

What happened

Press-reported

The sitting

On Sunday 11 Oct a special bench of Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana heard petitions that followed the 10 Oct protests at Jantar Mantar against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. The principal one was a contempt petition by advocate Shailendra Mani Tripathi over Delhi Metro station closures. Another was a habeas corpus petition by senior advocate Menaka Guruswamy over detentions. Related pleas were tagged to the contempt petition.

What it directed

As reported: no illegal detention, save and except by procedure established by law, particularly of peaceful, unarmed people beyond the area where Section 163 BNSS is in operation. Assembly permitted in accordance with law outside that area. Metro and rail to run normally unless regulations are required for public order under Article 19(2). Free movement subject to traffic regulations, barricades to be reviewed, internet undisturbed (IT Act powers not taken away). The notice board announcing Section 163 outside the Supreme Court to be taken down.

What it did not decide

Notice was issued to the alleged contemnors (named in the petition as the Union Home Secretary, the Delhi Police Commissioner and the DMRC chairperson) to file an affidavit and a compliance report. The press reports no immediate relief on the contempt allegations or on the release of detainees. The next date reported is Tuesday 13 Oct.

Built on

A 9 Oct order in M.A. Baby v Union of India, which the press says barred an outright prohibition on Metro movement.

The hub, in one line Reported: no detentions, traffic or metro restrictions except as per law. Section 163 BNSS. Article 19: speech and assembly.

PM IM II

A colonial power that survived the rebrand

Section 144 CrPC was the district magistrate's quickest tool against a crowd for well over a century. Since 1 July 2024 it lives on as Section 163 BNSS, and the board outside the Supreme Court shows how far it can stretch.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • CrPC s.144 to BNSS s.163 (Magistrate's urgent order; 2 months, State can extend to 6)
  • Babulal Parate v Maharashtra (1961)
  • Madhu Limaye v SDM Monghyr (1970-71): upheld, with limits
  • Anuradha Bhasin (2020): repeated orders are an abuse; orders must state facts and be reviewable
  • Disobedience: IPC 188 to BNS 223

Go deeper

Read the sub-clauses of s.163 side by side with old s.144 and see what Parliament actually changed.

Common confusion

Old section number and new section number are the usual swap. Also: the section is a power, the 'five persons' rule comes from the order.

PM IIE

Three words that took 28 years: 'procedure established by law'

Article 21 never changed. In 1950 (A.K. Gopalan) 'procedure established by law' meant any procedure a legislature had laid down. In 1978 (Maneka Gandhi) it came to mean a procedure that is fair, just and reasonable. The press reports the direction that there be no illegal detention 'save and except by procedure established by law', the same three words. (Tying the direction to that history is our reading, not the Court's.)

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Article 21 and the Gopalan to Maneka shift
  • Link to Articles 14 and 19: the 'golden triangle'
  • Usually traced to Japan's 1946 Constitution; the US phrase is 'due process'
  • Article 22: safeguards on arrest

Go deeper

Why did the framers pick Japan's wording over the American one, and who argued against it in the Constituent Assembly.

Common confusion

Procedure established by law is not the same as due process in text, yet India now reads it close to due process.

PM IIE

The only place the Constitution allows detention without trial in peacetime

Preventive detention sits inside the fundamental rights chapter itself. Police 'preventive arrest' of a crowd and preventive detention under a statute sound alike and are legally different animals.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Article 22(3)-(7): preventive detention, Advisory Board, 3 months
  • Police preventive arrest: CrPC s.151 to BNSS s.170
  • D.K. Basu (1997), Joginder Kumar (1994), Arnesh Kumar (2014): arrest safeguards
  • Habeas corpus: Articles 32 and 226
  • NSA 1980 as the statutory example

Go deeper

Follow one habeas corpus petition from filing to the High Court or Supreme Court. What is the Court actually allowed to order.

Common confusion

Hours-long police preventive arrest and months-long statutory preventive detention are mixed up constantly.

04
PM IIE

The right to gather has fewer exceptions than the right to speak

Speech can be limited on eight grounds. Assembly on two. Article 19(3) allows only sovereignty and integrity of India, and public order. That narrowness is why 'the area where 163 is in force' matters so much.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Article 19(1)(a) vs 19(1)(b) vs 19(1)(c)
  • Article 19(2) (eight grounds) vs 19(3) (two grounds)
  • 'Peaceably and without arms' is built into 19(1)(b)
  • Himat Lal Shah (1973): permission regimes
  • Re: Ramlila Maidan Incident (2012): right to assemble and protest is fundamental, can be regulated
  • Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (2018): Jantar Mantar, regulate not ban
  • Amit Sahni (2020): Shaheen Bagh, public roads cannot be occupied indefinitely

Go deeper

Read Ramlila Maidan and Amit Sahni back to back. One protects the protest, the other limits it. Where does each draw the line.

Common confusion

Article 19(2) is the speech clause. The press quotes the Court citing it for transport closures, so check which clause governs which freedom.

05
PM IIE

A 1950 protest case rewrote the Constitution's text

Romesh Thappar's magazine was banned in Madras. In the same year Brij Bhushan's journal faced a pre-censorship order in Delhi. The Court struck both down because 'public order' was not among the grounds for restricting speech. Parliament answered with the First Amendment (1951), adding 'public order' and 'reasonable'. Free-speech litigation changed the Constitution's text.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Romesh Thappar v Madras (1950) and Brij Bhushan v Delhi (1950)
  • Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951
  • Ram Manohar Lohia (1960): three circles, law and order, public order, security of State
  • Arun Ghosh (1970)
  • Article 19(2) 'public order'

Go deeper

Trace the three concentric circles and place the Delhi detentions in one of them.

Common confusion

Law and order, public order and security of State are different tiers; an order that is only 'law and order' cannot ride on the 19(2) public-order ground.

06
PM IIE

'Reasonable' became 'proportionate'

The old test asked if a restriction was reasonable. Courts now ask if it is suitable, necessary and the least restrictive means. Blanket Metro shutdowns and city-wide barricades are exactly the kind of measure that test is built to catch.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • State of Madras v V.G. Row (1952): early reasonableness test
  • Modern Dental College (2016) and Puttaswamy (2017): proportionality
  • Anuradha Bhasin (2020) applying it to shutdowns
  • Article 19(2)-(6): 'reasonable restrictions'

Go deeper

Apply the four-step proportionality test to any one restriction in the order and see which step fails first.

Common confusion

Proportionality is judge-made. It does not appear in the Article 19 text.

PM IIM III

The silence button: internet shutdowns

The Court said internet stays on while not taking away IT Act powers. India has been counted among the world's heaviest users of shutdowns in several recent years (Access Now, to be checked per year). The legal basis moved in 2023 from a colonial telegraph law to a new statute.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Anuradha Bhasin (2020): internet access through Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); orders to be published and reviewed
  • Telegraph Act 1885 and 2017 Suspension Rules to Telecommunications Act, 2023, s.20
  • IT Act s.69A (blocking) is a different power from shutdowns
  • GS-III: internal security and communication networks, social media
  • Cost of shutdowns to the economy

Go deeper

Find how many shutdowns India ordered in a recent year, and which State ordered the most.

Common confusion

Blocking a site (s.69A) and suspending a network are different powers under different laws.

PM II

Delhi is not a State, and that changes who is answerable

Delhi's elected government does not control Delhi Police. Under Article 239AA(3)(a) public order, police and land sit outside the Assembly's powers. That is the quiet reason the contempt notice names the Union Home Secretary and Delhi Police Commissioner next to the Metro chair. (The notice list is press-reported; the link to 239AA is our reading, not the Court's.)

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Article 239AA and the Seventh Schedule: public order (State List entry 1) and police (entry 2)
  • NCT of Delhi v Union of India (2018, Constitution Bench) and the 2023 services judgment
  • GNCTD (Amendment) Act, 2023
  • DMRC is a 50:50 Centre-Delhi company; Metro Railways Acts
  • Union deployment of forces: Union List entry 2A

Go deeper

Compare Delhi with Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir. Who controls police in each.

Common confusion

Delhi is a Union Territory with an Assembly, not a full State. Public order is a State subject, but not in Delhi.

PM IIE

When the State is the alleged contemnor

Contempt usually means a citizen defying a court. Here the notice goes to senior officials for allegedly ignoring a Metro order, and the Court held a Sunday sitting to deal with it. Article 32, which Ambedkar called the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution, is why anyone can come to this Court directly.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Articles 129 and 215: court of record, power to punish contempt
  • Contempt of Courts Act, 1971: civil vs criminal contempt
  • Article 141 (law declared binds all) and Article 142 (complete justice)
  • Article 32 and Ambedkar's 'heart and soul' remark
  • Access to justice: Article 39A, and the Section 163 board outside the Supreme Court

Go deeper

Find a past case where senior officials were held in contempt of the Supreme Court and what the Court ordered.

Common confusion

Civil and criminal contempt are defined differently; wilful disobedience of an order is civil contempt.

PM IM II

The contrast case: what an Emergency would change

Nothing in the press reports says Emergency was invoked, which is the point. In ordinary times every curb has to pass Article 19(3) and the Court's tests. Under a proclamation, Article 358 suspends Article 19 automatically, and Article 359 allows other rights to be suspended by Presidential order.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • Articles 352, 358, 359 and the 44th Amendment (1978): 'armed rebellion', Article 20-21 cannot be suspended
  • ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla (1976), repudiated in Puttaswamy (2017)
  • Article 358 triggers only for war or external aggression, not armed rebellion
  • Link to GS-I: the 1975-77 Emergency

Go deeper

Read why Justice H.R. Khanna dissented in ADM Jabalpur and what happened to his career.

Common confusion

Article 358 and Article 359 are routinely swapped. 358 is automatic for Article 19; 359 needs an order and covers other rights.

The object of the protest is itself a constitutional body

Press coverage says the protests were aimed at the Chief Election Commissioner. Whatever one thinks of the demand, the office is protected by design: the CEC can be removed only like a Supreme Court judge. (Protestors' allegations are not verified here and the layer takes no side.)

  • Article 324(5): removal protection for the CEC

Who decides a crowd has become a threat

Orders, barricades and mass detentions are executed by police, so the quality of policing is part of the story. The 1861 Police Act still shapes how forces are organised, and the Supreme Court ordered reforms in 2006 that States are still arguing over.

The street has a history: Rowlatt to JP

Protest is older than the Constitution. The Rowlatt agitation, the Salt March, Quit India and the 1974-75 JP movement all tested what a state may do to a crowd. The last of these ended in the Emergency.

Look abroad: time, place and manner

American courts allow governments to regulate when and where a protest happens but not what it says. Ramlila Maidan reads very much the same way. Europe protects assembly under Article 11 of the ECHR, and India is a party to the ICCPR, whose Article 21 protects peaceful assembly.

Static hooks, go deeper, common confusion

Static syllabus hooks not from the press; check the bare text

  • US First Amendment 'time, place and manner'
  • ECHR Article 11; ICCPR Article 21 (India a party)
  • UDHR Article 20
  • GS-II: comparison of constitutional schemes

Honesty note

  • Everything under “What happened” is press-reported (LiveLaw, Bar & Bench, The Hindu, SC Observer). The order text itself was not read on the Supreme Court site. Read the order before using any phrase from it in an answer.
  • The hub line “no detentions, traffic or metro restrictions except as per law” is the Bar & Bench headline. The body of the reports gives the direction as “no illegal detention, save and except by procedure established by law”. Neither is a quotation from the order itself, which was not read. The press quotes the order saying Metro and rail run normally “unless regulations are required” under Article 19(2).
  • Article 358 is a static link, not something the press reports the Court invoking. It appears in angle 10 as a contrast: what an Emergency would change.
  • “Bars assembly of five or more persons” is a press gloss on Section 163. The section is a Magistrate’s power to issue an order; the five-person figure comes from the order issued and from the unlawful-assembly offence (BNS 189).
  • Why people were protesting, and the legality of any detention, are allegations and contested claims in the coverage. This page treats them as context, not fact.
  • Static syllabus hooks (the anchors under each angle) come from standard syllabus knowledge and well-known judgments. Check clause numbers and section numbers against the bare text before using them. Old and new section numbers (CrPC to BNSS, IPC to BNS) are a known trap, so both are given.
