Where the rain goes: why cities flood in minutes

Less to read. More to connect. The rain has stopped. So why is the street still under water? On open ground much of a storm soaks in; in a city of roads, roofs and parking lots, far less of it can - and the water that cannot soak in has minutes, not days, to reach a drain. This is urban flooding, and it is a different disaster from a river in spate.

A puzzle first, a term later. Engineers size drains with a simple product - a runoff coefficient, a rainfall intensity, a catchment area - yet the same street can still flood after the drain is doubled. Five questions on the mechanism, the measurement, the fixes, and the 2010 NDMA guidelines that first treated urban flooding as a disaster of its own.

Fifteen connected angles

01 · 01 - HOOK

Same streets, new weather

Yesterday these roads and roofs were storing the day's heat. Today the monsoon asks them a second question: where does the rain go? On open ground, much of a storm soaks in. On concrete, bitumen and rooftops, far less of it can - and that single difference is why a city street can flood while the rain is still falling.

02 · 02 - CONCEPT

Urban flooding is not riverine flooding

Riverine floods typically build more slowly as rivers swell. Urban flooding is a different disaster: the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) notes that developed catchments can raise flood peaks from 1.8 to 8 times and flood volumes by up to 6 times, and because water travels faster over pavement than over soil, flooding can arrive in a matter of minutes. That is why the NDMA says its 2010 guidelines addressed urban flooding as a separate disaster for the first time, delinked from floods generally.

03 · 03 - MECHANISM

Impervious surfaces: the first domino

Roads, parking lots and rooftops are impervious - water cannot pass through them. The US Geological Survey (USGS) explains the chain: as a watershed urbanises, the area where rain can infiltrate shrinks, so far more stormwater runs off and must be collected by drains that carry it to streams. More water arrives in the stream, more quickly - raising the odds of more frequent and more severe flooding.

04 · 04 - MECHANISM

Time of concentration: why minutes matter

Engineers call the time a catchment needs to deliver stormwater to an outlet its 'time of concentration'. Vegetated, pervious ground slows that delivery; a paved catchment shortens it. The 2010 guidelines' sample flood-characterisation system - used for early-warning decision support - normally takes the rainfall duration at least equal to the catchment's time of concentration, because the storm that matters is the short, intense one that arrives all at once.

05 · 05 - NUANCE

It is the intensity, not the total

A year's rain spread evenly rarely floods anyone; the same total falling in a day is a disaster. The USGS makes this point with a simple contrast: a region getting 50 inches a year averages an inch a week, but a single storm can dump a large share of it at once. The NDMA's comparison is sharper for India: Mumbai's average July rainfall alone is 868 mm, more than London's entire annual average of 611 mm - historical averages that show what Indian monsoon cities have to cope with.

06 · 06 - MEASUREMENT

Design storm and return period

Drains are not built for the worst storm imaginable but for a chosen 'design storm' - a rainfall event of specified amount, intensity, duration and frequency. Its 'return period' is an average, not a schedule: a once-in-2-years design storm is one expected to be equalled or exceeded on average once in two years - about a 50% chance in any given year - and two such storms can arrive in consecutive years. The MoHUA manual recommends designing ordinary urban catchments for once-in-2-years storms in smaller cities and once-in-5-years in Class I cities; a larger design return period means a bigger design storm. An older drainage manual mentioned in the NDMA guidelines used a uniform design intensity of 12-20 mm/h for all cities, ignoring how rainfall varies across and within them - and the NDMA notes drains often under-perform anyway from poor maintenance.

07 · 07 - MEASUREMENT

Q = CIA: the rational method

The workhorse of storm-drain sizing is the rational method. The MoHUA manual writes it as Qp = 10 x C x I x A: peak flow in cubic metres per hour equals ten times C (a dimensionless runoff coefficient between 0 and 1), times I (the average rainfall intensity in mm/h over a duration equal to the time of concentration), times A (the catchment area in hectares). Urbanisation pushes C up, and the 2010 guidelines direct that future drains be designed with C up to 0.95, taking into account the approved land-use pattern. The method is meant for small catchments - the manual caps them at about 8-10 sq km and sub-divides larger ones - and it assumes the whole catchment contributes at once. For the same rain over the same area, the peak the drain must carry grows with C, I and A alike.

08 · 08 - MECHANISM

Drains that cannot carry their design load

Even a correctly sized drain fails if its capacity is eaten away. The NDMA lists encroachments that narrow natural drains and solid waste dumped into channels; the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA) operation-and-maintenance manual adds siltation, blocked cross-sections and illegal encroachment of drainage corridors. A drain's paper capacity and its real capacity on a monsoon afternoon can be very different numbers.

09 · 09 - CONCEPT

Detention, retention, recharge: three different fixes

The 2010 NDMA glossary draws distinctions Prelims loves: detention holds the extra runoff temporarily and releases it at a slower rate than it is collected, reducing the peak outflow. Retention collects and holds runoff with no surface outflow at all. Recharge sends water down to replenish groundwater - natural when unaided, artificial when engineered. Confusing 'hold and release slower' with 'remove' is a classic exam trap.

10 · 10 - CASE STUDY

2010: urban flooding gets its own playbook

Mumbai's July 2005 floods were, in the NDMA's words, an eye-opener: past flood strategy had focused on riverine floods in rural areas. The NDMA says its 2010 Management of Urban Flooding guidelines were the first time urban flooding was addressed as a separate disaster, delinked from floods generally, with catchment-based drainage design and watershed-based management. The guidelines are a framework on paper - evidence of doctrine, not of implementation in any particular city.

11 · 11 - INSTITUTION

Whose drain is it anyway?

A raindrop falling on one side of a ward boundary does not respect it. Stormwater crosses many jurisdictions before it reaches an outfall, and the MoHUA's manuals flag a multiplicity of agencies with overlapping responsibilities as a core maintenance problem. The guidelines' answer is planning at the catchment scale for design and the watershed scale for management - not the administrative-ward scale: the water does not know whose jurisdiction it is in.

12 · 12 - POLICY

Sponging it back: the mitigation toolbox

The engineering response runs two ways: convey water better (free-flowing cross-sections, maintained outfalls, encroachments removed) or sink it at source (infiltration trenches and recharge structures, both in the MoHUA manual). The stakes are doubled: the manual warns that rising imperviousness cuts groundwater recharge and lowers the water table - so stormwater lost as runoff is also water security lost.

13 · 13 - MAINS USE

A GS-III frame in one line

Urban flooding is a governance disaster wearing a weather costume: the hazard is rain, but the risk is built - impervious growth, encroached drains, split agencies and under-designed infrastructure. That single frame - hazard versus built risk - carries most GS-III answers on urban floods, disaster management and urban governance.

14 · 14 - ESSAY SEED

The city that deleted its sponge

Every city begins by removing much of the landscape's ability to absorb water, then spends decades engineering substitutes for what it removed. Keep this as a theme line: development as a bargain with the water cycle - and what renegotiating it looks like.

15 · 15 - ONE NEXT READ

Surat 2006, on both lists

Surat's 2006 floods appear on the NDMA's and the MoHUA manual's lists of notable urban floods. One documented event, read from primary sources, teaches more about how cities and rivers interact than a month of headlines. (It is a documented past event, not a comment on any current flood.)

Five-question Prelims challenge

The companion reels have no answers. Choose an option here to reveal the correct answer and explanation. These are original practice questions, not past UPSC questions.

Question 1

After an intense monsoon burst, the streets of a built-up city can flood within minutes, while open countryside nearby shows no flooding. Which of the following best explains this difference?

Select the best explanation.

Correct answer: C

Urbanisation replaces infiltrating soil and vegetation with impervious surfaces. The USGS describes the result: more stormwater arrives in drains and streams, more quickly. The NDMA quantifies it - developed catchments can raise flood peaks 1.8 to 8 times and volumes up to 6 times. Option A describes riverine flooding, which the NDMA explicitly separates. Options B and D fail as blanket claims: the NDMA attributes the minutes-scale onset to faster flow times from developed catchments, and only separately notes that urban heat islands may increase rainfall somewhat over cities - it does not present extra rainfall as the cause of the minutes-scale flooding.

Source: NDMA, Urban Floods overview (developed catchments can raise flood peaks 1.8-8 times and volumes up to 6 times; faster flow over pavement; urban heat island may increase rainfall over urban areas); USGS Water Science School, Impervious Surfaces and Flooding (more stormwater reaches streams faster)

Question 2

With reference to how urbanisation changes the movement of rainwater, consider the following statements:

  1. Impervious surfaces reduce the area available for infiltration, which also reduces groundwater recharge.
  2. A developed catchment delivers stormwater to streams more slowly than a forested one, because drains regulate the flow.
  3. Detention permanently removes runoff from the drainage system.
  4. Retention collects and holds runoff with no surface outflow.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Correct answer: A

Statement 1 is correct - both the USGS and MoHUA describe reduced infiltration cutting recharge and lowering the water table. Statement 2 is wrong: urban catchments deliver water faster, so the peak arrives earlier and higher, not later. Statement 3 is wrong: detention holds the extra runoff temporarily and releases it at a slower rate, reducing the peak - it does not remove the runoff. Statement 4 is correct - the NDMA's 2010 glossary defines retention as holding runoff with no surface outflow.

Source: USGS, Impervious Surfaces and Flooding and Infiltration and the Water Cycle; MoHUA Stormwater Drainage Manual 2019, engineering volume (imperviousness cuts groundwater recharge); NDMA Management of Urban Flooding guidelines 2010, glossary (detention releases slower, retention has no surface outflow)

Question 3

Consider the following pairs of stormwater measures and the mechanisms they rely on:

  1. Infiltration trench : restores on-site soaking of runoff into the ground
  2. Detention pond : permanently removes runoff from the drainage system
  3. Clearing encroachments and dumped waste from natural drains : restores some of the conveyance capacity the drain originally had

How many of the above pairs are correctly matched?

Correct answer: B

Pairs 1 and 3 hold: infiltration trenches sink water at source (MoHUA engineering manual), and encroachments and dumped debris shrink a drain's real capacity below its design capacity, so clearing them restores conveyance (NDMA; MoHUA O&M manual). Pair 2 is the trap: detention holds runoff temporarily and releases it slower - it reduces the peak outflow but removes nothing.

Source: MoHUA Stormwater Drainage Manual 2019, engineering volume (infiltration trenches exfiltrate to subsoil) and O&M volume (siltation, solid-waste blockage, illegal encroachment of drainage corridors); NDMA 2010 guidelines glossary (detention reduces the peak outflow but removes nothing)

Question 4

With reference to the NDMA's 2010 guidelines on urban flooding, consider the following statements:

  1. The NDMA describes them as the first time urban flooding was addressed as a separate disaster, delinked from riverine floods.
  2. They recommend that drainage be planned at the level of administrative wards, since municipal bodies own the drains.
  3. Under them, the catchment is the basis for drainage design and the watershed the basis for management actions.
  4. In their sample flood-characterisation system, the rainfall duration is normally taken at least equal to the catchment's time of concentration.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Correct answer: D

Statement 1 is correct - the NDMA says its 2010 guidelines, prompted by the 2005 Mumbai floods, addressed urban flooding as a separate disaster for the first time. Statement 2 is wrong: the guidelines call for catchment and watershed-based planning precisely because water ignores ward and agency boundaries. Statement 3 is correct - the guidelines assign the catchment as the basis of design and the watershed as the basis of management. Statement 4 is correct - their sample flood-characterisation system normally takes the rainfall duration at least equal to the time of concentration.

Source: NDMA Management of Urban Flooding guidelines 2010 (NDMA's first treatment of urban flooding as a separate disaster, delinked from riverine floods; catchment as the basis of drainage design, watershed as the basis of management; sample flood-characterisation system, duration normally at least the time of concentration)

Question 5

A city doubles the width of a major stormwater drain downstream. In the next monsoon, a ward upstream still floods during short, intense bursts of rain. Consider the following inferences:

  1. Continued paving and building upstream may have raised the runoff peak beyond even the enlarged capacity.
  2. The runoff coefficient of a surface depends mainly on the intensity of the rainfall falling on it.
  3. Siltation and dumped waste in the drain, or an outfall constrained by high water in the receiving stream, can throttle even an enlarged drain.
  4. The season's total rainfall was close to normal, so the flooding points to drainage failure alone and not to anything about the rainfall itself.

Which of the inferences given above is/are valid?

Correct answer: B

Inference 1 is valid: the rational method (Q = CIA) shows a higher runoff coefficient upstream raises the peak for the same rain; the NDMA warns development keeps raising flows. Inference 2 is the trap: the coefficient C describes the surface, not the rainfall - intensity enters separately as I. Inference 3 is valid: the MoHUA manuals document siltation and waste blockage cutting real capacity below design, and a tail-water-constrained outfall limits discharge however wide the drain. Inference 4 fails: urban flooding depends on intensity and duration, not seasonal totals - a short intense burst can overwhelm drains in a statistically normal monsoon.

Source: NDMA Urban Floods overview and 2010 guidelines (development keeps raising flows; runoff coefficient reflects the nature of the surface); MoHUA engineering manual 2019 (Qp = 10 C I A; tail-water design at outfalls); MoHUA O&M manual 2019 (siltation and solid-waste blockage)
