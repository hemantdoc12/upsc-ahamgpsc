Drinking-water quality · Five questions, fifteen connected angles

Clear is not a safety certificate: reading drinking-water evidence

Two samples can look equally clear and still answer very different questions. Follow the evidence from aquifer to household, separate microbial and chemical hazards, and learn what a test can establish without claiming more than it measured.

Read this as a reasoning lesson. Examples are hypothetical. No real locality is diagnosed. No numerical drinking-water limits, current coverage claims or household treatment instructions are given. Source documents and dates are identified below.

Fifteen connections

01 / 15

Clear is an observation, not a verdict

Appearance tells us something about visible material, not everything about safety. Water containing germs may look clean. Dissolved constituents may also be unseen. Start with the question a measurement can answer, rather than treating clear and safe as interchangeable words.

Source: CDC CLEAR

Additional source: USGS QUALITY

02 / 15

One parameter, one bounded finding

An E. coli non-detection result is useful evidence about that parameter in that sample. It is not a finding that every organism and chemical is absent. The right response to limited evidence is to identify what remains unchecked, not to discard the evidence or inflate its scope.

Source: WHO TEMPORAL

03 / 15

Natural is not the same as harmless

Water interacts with the geological materials through which it moves. Naturally occurring arsenic and fluoride can be important groundwater hazards. Natural origin does not establish safety; equally, the possibility of a natural hazard does not diagnose a particular well. That needs appropriate evidence.

Source: WHO WATER

04 / 15

Different hazards enter at different points

A hazard need not begin inside the borewell. WHO identifies lead leaching from supply components in contact with water. CDC lists fertilisers and malfunctioning septic systems among possible nitrate sources. The explanatory task is to trace a plausible source and pathway, then test it, not assume all contamination has one cause.

Source: CDC CHEMICALS

Additional source: WHO WATER

05 / 15

Follow water beyond the source

Good source quality does not guarantee the quality of the water eventually consumed. Withdrawal, transport and household storage are possible points of recontamination. A source-to-consumer diagram is therefore a map of places to examine, not proof that a specific container or pipe caused a positive result.

Source: WHO GROUNDWATER

06 / 15

Time belongs on the sample label

Contamination can vary over time. A source sample collected today does not reconstruct the condition of a batch collected yesterday. Put the sampling time beside the result. Otherwise, a comparison may look like a controlled before-and-after test even though it compares different batches.

Source: WHO TEMPORAL

07 / 15

Ask what comparison the samples allow

A household result and a source result may reveal a problem worth investigating without locating its exact entry point. An earlier contaminated source batch and post-collection contamination can both remain possible. Preserve those alternatives until evidence distinguishes them. This is causal reasoning, not indecision.

Source: WHO GROUNDWATER

08 / 15

Microbial inactivation is not chemical removal

A treatment effective against germs is not automatically effective against dissolved chemicals. Boiling and disinfection are not general assurances of safety when harmful chemical contamination is present. In an exam question, identify the hazard and the process before accepting a treatment claim. This lesson does not provide a household treatment recipe.

Source: CDC CLEAR

09 / 15

A filter is a technology, not a universal promise

Different filtration technologies have different capabilities. Removing a class of organisms does not establish removal of dissolved chemicals. For reverse osmosis, check the particular system and contaminant-specific performance claim rather than assuming the name of the technology guarantees every outcome. Correct operation, maintenance and appropriate verification still matter.

Source: CDC HOME

10 / 15

Two kinds of hazard do not cancel

A satisfactory result for one parameter cannot cancel an exceedance for another. In the hypothetical question, E. coli non-detection at the source does not erase the stipulated fluoride finding. Nor does identifying a chemical issue explain away a microbial finding in stored water. Keep the findings separate before combining the response.

Source: USGS QUALITY

11 / 15

Prevent failure, not only detect it

WHO describes water safety plans as a preventive risk-management approach encompassing all steps in the supply. Testing is one source of evidence, but the framework also asks how risks are identified and managed before people are exposed. A final sample is not a substitute for a functioning safety system.

Source: WHO FAQ

12 / 15

Targets, plans and oversight have different jobs

WHO combines health-based targets and standards, supplier water safety plans, and independent surveillance. These are complementary functions. A target defines what is sought; a plan manages risks across delivery; independent surveillance checks that the arrangements and outcomes meet the intended protection. Do not turn this into a claim that WHO guidelines themselves are Indian law.

Source: WHO FAQ

13 / 15

JJM monitoring is not laboratory work alone

The December 2019 Jal Jeevan Mission Operational Guidelines define water-quality monitoring to include laboratory and field testing of source and household-tap samples by the responsible rural water-supply agency. The inclusion of field testing matters: a simple laboratory-versus-community binary misstates the definition. This is an attributed definition from that dated document.

Source: JJM OPERATIONAL

14 / 15

Community surveillance adds, rather than replaces

The same JJM document describes surveillance involving communities, Gram Panchayats and their committees, or schools, using field kits and similar assessments. This does not replace the agency monitoring role. Also keep terminology separate: WHO independent surveillance and JJM community surveillance are not asserted here to be identical institutional arrangements.

Source: JJM OPERATIONAL

15 / 15

The Mains and essay bridge: evidence and accountability

A useful governance answer connects hazard-specific evidence with responsibilities across the supply chain. Who manages the risk? Who tests, where and when? Who independently checks the system? What remains unknown? The wider essay lesson is that an attractive surface can conceal an incomplete measurement: confidence should grow from evidence and a working system, not from appearance alone.

Source: WHO FAQ

Five Prelims questions

Choose an option to reveal the key and every option's explanation. Questions 1-2 form Prelims 1/3, 3-4 form Prelims 2/3, and question 5 is the integrated application in Prelims 3/3.

Question 1 / 5

A borewell sample looks clear. A laboratory reports that Escherichia coli (E. coli) was not detected in that sample. No chemical analysis has been done. Which conclusion is best supported?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. A result for one microbial indicator cannot establish the absence of untested chemicals or all other microbes throughout a supply.

B. Correct. Keep the conclusion within the sample and parameter actually tested. Appearance and an E. coli non-detection result do not settle chemical suitability or establish the absence of all other microbial hazards.

C. Wrong. Groundwater can contain harmful dissolved chemicals, but possibility is not evidence that this borewell does. Appropriate analysis is still needed.

D. Wrong. Bounded evidence is useful. Its limits do not make it worthless or require simultaneous testing of every imaginable contaminant.

Question 2 / 5

Consider these possible contaminant-pathway pairs:
1. Arsenic or fluoride: release from geological materials into groundwater.
2. Lead: leaching from water-supply components in contact with water.
3. Nitrate: entry associated with fertiliser use or malfunctioning septic systems.
How many pairs are correctly matched?

Correct answer: C

A. Wrong. Each of the three describes a possible pathway; the pairs are not claims that these are the only sources.

B. Wrong. None needs to be excluded. Arsenic and fluoride can be geogenic, lead can enter from supply components, and nitrate can be associated with fertilisers or septic contamination.

C. Correct. The location at which a hazard enters can differ: the aquifer, the catchment or subsurface flow path, or the supply infrastructure.

D. Wrong. All three mechanisms are recognised. Natural origin does not itself establish safety, and contamination need not originate at the borewell.

Pair 1: Groundwater can acquire arsenic or fluoride through interaction with geological materials; natural origin does not establish safety.

Pair 2: Lead can enter water through leaching from supply components, so hazards can arise after abstraction.

Pair 3: Fertiliser use and malfunctioning septic systems are recognised possible nitrate sources; a pathway is not a diagnosis of a particular well.

Question 3 / 5

With reference to drinking-water treatment, consider these statements:
1. Boiling that addresses microbial hazards also establishes removal of dissolved arsenic.
2. A filter's ability to remove one class of organisms does not establish its ability to remove dissolved chemicals.
3. Reverse-osmosis performance for a particular chemical should be checked against the system's specified removal capability.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: D

A. Wrong. Statement 2 is correct, but statement 3 must also be retained. A particular reverse-osmosis system must be evaluated for the chemical concerned; the technology name is not a universal performance guarantee.

B. Wrong. Statement 3 is correct, but statement 2 is also correct. Removing organisms does not by itself establish removal of dissolved chemicals. Different hazards require different capabilities.

C. Wrong. Statements 2 and 3 are correct, but statement 1 confuses microbial inactivation with arsenic removal. Boiling does not establish removal of dissolved arsenic.

D. Correct. Both the limited inference from filter capability and the contaminant-specific performance check are sound. A device label is not a substitute for correct operation, maintenance and appropriate performance verification.

Question 4 / 5

Consider the following statements about managing drinking-water safety:
1. WHO's framework combines health-based targets, preventive water safety plans and independent surveillance.
2. Under the December 2019 Jal Jeevan Mission Operational Guidelines, agency monitoring includes laboratory and field testing of source and household-tap samples.
3. Those guidelines assign community field-kit surveillance the role of replacing agency monitoring.
Which statements are correct?

Correct answer: A

A. Correct. WHO combines targets, preventive water safety plans and independent surveillance. The December 2019 JJM definitions include both laboratory and field testing in agency monitoring, alongside community surveillance as a distinct activity.

B. Wrong. Statement 2 is correct, but statement 1 also belongs. WHO explicitly describes the three complementary functions; testing alone is not the whole preventive framework.

C. Wrong. Statement 1 is correct, but statement 2 also belongs. The dated JJM definition includes laboratory AND field testing by the responsible agency, not laboratory work alone.

D. Wrong. It includes the invented replacement claim in statement 3. Community surveillance does not abolish agency monitoring. WHO independent surveillance and JJM community surveillance are not asserted to be identical institutional arrangements.

Question 5 / 5

In a hypothetical village, a household stores water collected from its borewell supply the previous evening. Properly collected samples taken today show fluoride above the applicable limit at the source, with E. coli not detected there; the household storage sample contains E. coli. Both look clear.
Consider these inferences:
1. Source E. coli non-detection cancels the fluoride exceedance as a safety concern.
2. Contamination after collection is possible, but contamination of the earlier source batch is not ruled out.
3. Boiling alone resolves both the fluoride and microbial findings.
4. Chemical-hazard control and the control and investigation of microbial contamination are distinct needs.
5. The results establish that the source was free of E. coli when the household collected its water.
Which inferences are correct?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. It adds inference 1, the parameter-override mistake. An E. coli non-detection result cannot cancel a fluoride exceedance; the results address different hazards.

B. Correct. Inference 2 keeps both temporal and post-collection explanations open: stored water came from an earlier source batch, not the water sampled at the source today. Contamination during collection, transport or storage is also possible. Inference 4 recognises distinct chemical and microbial needs.

C. Wrong. It adds inference 3, the treatment-substitution mistake. Microbial inactivation by boiling does not establish removal of dissolved fluoride.

D. Wrong. It adds inference 5, the time-matching mistake. Today's source sample does not establish the source condition when the stored batch was collected yesterday. These are not paired samples of the same water parcel.
