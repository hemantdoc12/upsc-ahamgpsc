Why the city stays warm at night: urban heat islands

Less to read. More to connect. The sun has set. The fields outside the city are cooling fast - but the road outside your door, the roof above you and the parking lot down the street are still giving back the day's heat. Tonight your city is an island of warmth in a cooler countryside. This is the urban heat island, and it changes how cities must be planned.

A puzzle first, a term later. Concrete stores more of the day's energy than dry soil and lets it go slowly through the night; satellites map the skin of the city while weather stations measure the air people breathe - and the two are not the same number. Five questions on the mechanism, the measurement, the fixes, and the Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan that showed a city can fight back.

Fifteen connected angles

01 · Hook

The sunset puzzle

The sun has set. The open ground outside the city cools quickly. But the road, the roof and the parking lot are still radiating warmth, and the city's air stays hotter than the countryside around it. This everyday observation is the urban heat island: the city as an island of heat surrounded by a cooler rural sea.

02 · Concept

Relative, not absolute

An urban heat island is a temperature difference between a city and its surroundings - not a fixed temperature, not a heat wave, and not the same thing as global warming. A city can show a heat island in any season, day or night; a heat wave is a spell of extreme absolute temperature; and climate change is a separate, planet-scale trend that makes heat waves more frequent. The three interact, but they are not interchangeable words.

03 · Mechanism

Where the heat comes from

Four drivers stack up. First, urban materials: conventional roads, roofs and walls reflect less sunlight (lower albedo) and have heat capacities that let them soak up daytime energy and release it slowly. Second, reduced natural landscapes: hard dry surfaces replace the shade and moisture that trees, vegetation and water bodies provide. Third, urban geometry: tall buildings and narrow streets form urban canyons that trap heat and block cooling winds. Fourth, waste heat: vehicles, air-conditioners, factories and human activity pump heat directly into the urban air.

04 · Mechanism

Why nights differ

The heat island often becomes more pronounced after sunset - and this is specifically an air-temperature story. The US EPA notes that surface temperatures vary far more than air temperatures during the day, while surface and air temperatures are generally similar at night. Rural dry soil and sand heat up fast by day but also cool fast at night; urban materials - stone, concrete, steel - store more of the day's energy and radiate it back through the evening, warming the canopy-layer air where people live. So the city can be only modestly warmer at noon yet clearly warmer at midnight. This reduced night-time cooling of the air is also where much of the health stress lies: bodies get no recovery window. (The daytime surface picture - angle 06 - is a separate, sharper contrast.)

05 · Nuance

The dry-rural exception

The city-hotter pattern is not universal. In arid regions, dry rural surfaces with little vegetation can heat up more than city surfaces during the day - and irrigated city greenery can even cool a city below its dry surroundings. That is why scientists speak of heat islands as localised, time-specific and season-specific differences, and why a single satellite snapshot is not a verdict.

06 · Measurement

Surface heat islands: what satellites see

Thermal sensors on satellites such as Landsat and MODIS estimate land surface temperature - the temperature of the ground, roofs and roads themselves, measured in Kelvin. Surface heat islands are most intense during the day: on a warm day a conventional rooftop can run far hotter than the air around it. A satellite therefore maps the skin of the city, not the air its residents breathe.

07 · Measurement

Atmospheric heat islands: what satellites do not see

The air above a city has its own heat islands, in two layers: the canopy layer, from ground level to roughly the tops of buildings and trees, where people live; and the boundary layer, the thicker slab of atmosphere above. Canopy-layer temperatures are measured by instruments at weather stations or by mobile traverses through city streets; the boundary layer needs tall towers, radiosondes or aircraft. Atmospheric heat islands vary less in intensity than surface ones, but they are what human health actually feels.

08 · Measurement

Reading a heat map honestly

Satellite land surface temperature is a radiometric estimate of the surface itself; a weather-station thermometer measures air temperature. These are different quantities. Checking a satellite LST product against ground truth therefore means comparing it with measurements of the ground surface - ground-based thermal radiometry - not with the air reading from a nearby station. And a scorching rooftop reading does not mean the air inside the homes below is equally hot: indoor and street-level air depend on ventilation, shading and building design. Satellite maps are excellent at finding where surfaces run hot; converting that into resident exposure takes ground data and care.

09 · Mitigation

Matching fixes to mechanisms

Each driver has a counterpart. Cool roofs use high-reflectivity surfaces to send sunlight back before it becomes stored heat. Urban trees work twice: they shade surfaces directly and cool the air through evapotranspiration - the release of water vapour from leaves. Green roofs add both insulation and moisture. Cool or permeable pavements, ventilation corridors that respect wind flow, and cuts in waste heat complete the toolkit. The benefits are site-specific: no honest plan promises a fixed number of degrees everywhere.

10 · Case study

Ahmedabad: South Asia's first Heat Action Plan (historic)

After a severe heat wave in 2010 - Azhar et al. (2014) later estimated 1,344 additional deaths that May - the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation and partners piloted the city's first Heat Action Plan in 2013, described in its 2018 evaluation as South Asia's first, with full implementation beginning in 2014. The plan paired colour-coded early warnings - issued by the AMC through a nodal officer, using forecasts from the India Meteorological Department's Ahmedabad centre and thresholds derived from retrospective analysis of the city's own temperature-mortality data - with hospital training, public outreach through WhatsApp, radio and pamphlets, cooling centres and water distribution, and longer-term measures such as the Cool Roofs Program (white lime-wash pilots reached about 3,000 low-income homes in 2017-18; in the right setting cool roofs can keep indoor temperatures about 2 to 5 degrees Celsius lower than traditional roofs). The 2018 evaluation compared the 2014-2015 heat seasons against a 2007-2010 baseline and estimated about 2,380 fewer deaths than pre-plan mortality rates predicted (95% CI 324-4,435; about 1,190 per year) - an ecological association with wide uncertainty, not a controlled result, but one of the first effectiveness findings for heat action plans anywhere.

11 · Case study

Ahmedabad from orbit (2021 study)

A 2021 study of Ahmedabad using MODIS thermal data from 2003 to 2018 mapped the city's surface heat island over sixteen years. It found the heat island's spatial extent spread uniformly through the city while heat accumulated in the dense central core, in both summer and winter - and that the overall increase in surface heat island magnitude over the period was most profoundly visible in the winter season. The finding is a historical research result, not a current-temperature claim, but it underlines that heat islands are a year-round urban-form issue, not only a summer emergency.

12 · Health

Who is at risk

Heat islands raise daytime peaks and, just as importantly, cut night-time relief - and lost night-time cooling is a recognised driver of heat stress, because bodies get no recovery window. The burden falls hardest on older adults, young children, outdoor workers, low-income communities in heat-trapping housing, and people with chronic illness or limited mobility. That vulnerability pattern, documented by the US EPA and built into Ahmedabad's planning, is universal even though the numbers behind it vary by country.

13 · Economy

Energy, water, ecosystems

Hotter cities demand more cooling: research reviewed by the US EPA shows electricity demand for air-conditioning rising measurably with every increment of urban warming, peaking on the hottest afternoons and straining grids exactly when they are most stressed. Less obviously, overheated roofs and pavements heat stormwater runoff, which then warms the streams and rivers it enters; studies of urban streams find sharper temperature surges than in forested areas, stressing aquatic life.

14 · Policy

From maps to plans

The planning chain taught in NASA's applied trainings runs: map land surface temperature from satellites, layer on where vulnerable people actually live - age, income, health, housing - to build heat vulnerability indices, then aim tree cover, cool roofs and warning systems at the worst overlaps. Ahmedabad's documented plan demonstrates the response half of such a chain - early warning, inter-agency coordination, hospital preparedness, outreach and cool roofs.

15 · What to watch

Honest limits of the science

Three cautions for any headline. A heat island intensity is always relative to a chosen rural reference, so numbers differ with how the comparison is drawn. Satellite estimates cannot see through cloud, and their surface readings are not street-level air. And projections - like the Ahmedabad study's estimate of heat island magnitude in 2034 under different vegetation and albedo scenarios - are scenario exercises, not forecasts. The science is strongest as a targeting tool, weakest when read as a single dramatic number.

Five-question Prelims challenge

The companion reels have no answers. Choose an option here to reveal the correct answer and explanation. These are original practice questions, not past UPSC questions.

Question 1

The sun has set over a large city, yet its streets stay warmer than the surrounding countryside. Which of the following best explains why urban areas often cool more slowly at night?

Select the best explanation.

Correct answer: A

Option A is correct: urban materials such as steel and stone have higher heat capacities than rural materials like dry soil and sand, so cities store more of the day's solar energy and release it slowly through the night - the EPA's compendium notes downtown areas can absorb and store twice the heat of their rural surroundings by day. Option B fails: rural areas do not systematically receive less solar radiation; the difference lies in how surfaces handle the same energy, not in how much arrives. Option C fails: greenhouse gas concentrations do not spike over cities at sunset; that confuses the local heat island with the separate, global greenhouse effect. Option D fails: the night-time contrast is no instrument artifact - it is measured directly by stations sited in both urban and rural areas, and satellites add an independent quantity (surface temperature) that shows its own heat-island pattern; the two measurements observe different things, and neither makes the effect vanish.

Source: US EPA, Reducing Urban Heat Islands: Compendium of Strategies (urban materials' higher heat capacity; night-time slow release); NASA ARSET UHI training Q&A, Part 1 (Q7: night-time release; arid dry-rural exception)

Question 2

With reference to how scientists measure urban heat islands, consider the following statements:

  1. Satellites such as Landsat and MODIS estimate land surface temperature, not the temperature of the air.
  2. Surface heat islands and atmospheric heat islands are two names for the same measurement.
  3. Canopy-layer temperatures - the air people actually experience at street level - are measured by instruments at weather stations or by mobile traverses.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Correct answer: B

Statement 1 is correct: thermal satellite sensors estimate the temperature of surfaces - roofs, roads, ground - expressed in Kelvin, not air temperature. Statement 2 is incorrect: surface and atmospheric heat islands differ in how they form, how they are measured and their impacts; atmospheric heat islands sit in the canopy and boundary layers of the air and are measured with stations, traverses, towers, radiosondes and aircraft. Statement 3 is correct: canopy-layer air temperature, the layer people live in, comes from in-situ instruments at meteorological stations or mobile surveys, not directly from satellites.

Source: NASA ARSET UHI training Q&A, Part 1 (Q38: satellites estimate LST; canopy measured in situ, boundary layer via towers/radiosondes/aircraft); US EPA, What Are Heat Islands (surface vs atmospheric heat islands)

Question 3

Consider the following pairs of heat island mitigation measures and the mechanisms they rely on:

  1. Cool roofs : high reflectivity sends incoming sunlight back before it is absorbed as heat
  2. Urban trees : shade surfaces directly and cool the air through evapotranspiration from leaves
  3. Urban water bodies : water's high heat capacity keeps its surface temperature fairly steady between day and night, unlike pavement

How many of the above pairs are correctly matched?

Correct answer: C

All three pairs are correctly matched. Cool roofs work through reflectivity (albedo) - they send incoming sunlight back rather than absorbing and storing it as heat; where a material also has high emissivity, that separately helps it re-emit any heat it has absorbed. Trees cool twice over: by shading surfaces and by evapotranspiration, the evaporation of water from leaves, which carries heat away. Water bodies matter through heat capacity: the EPA's compendium notes that water maintains a fairly constant temperature day and night because of its high heat capacity, in contrast to the dips and spikes of built surfaces. These are the standard mechanisms in the EPA's heat island guidance; Ahmedabad's Cool Roofs Program applies the first of them.

Source: US EPA, What Are Heat Islands (albedo; trees - shade and evapotranspiration); US EPA, Reducing Urban Heat Islands: Compendium of Strategies (water's high heat capacity, fairly constant day-night temperature); Ahmedabad HAP 2019 easy-read (Cool Roofs Program)

Question 4

With reference to Ahmedabad's Heat Action Plan and its evaluation (a historic case study), consider the following statements:

  1. It was piloted in 2013, after a severe heat wave in 2010, and fully implemented from 2014.
  2. The plan's alert thresholds were developed without reference to Ahmedabad's own temperature-mortality experience.
  3. A 2018 evaluation estimated about 2,380 fewer deaths in 2014-2015 than pre-plan mortality rates would have predicted.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Correct answer: B

Statements 1 and 3 are correct. Statement 1: after the 2010 heat wave - Azhar et al. (2014) estimated 1,344 additional deaths that May - the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation and partners piloted the city's first Heat Action Plan in 2013, which its 2018 evaluation (Hess et al.) describes as South Asia's first, with full implementation beginning in 2014. Statement 2 is incorrect: the thresholds were built precisely on local evidence - retrospective epidemiological analyses of health-relevant temperature thresholds for Ahmedabad. IMD's Ahmedabad centre supplies the forecasts and the AMC (through its nodal officer) issues the alerts, but the thresholds themselves rest on the city's own temperature-mortality data. Statement 3 is correct: the 2018 evaluation, comparing the 2014-2015 heat seasons against a 2007-2010 baseline, estimated about 2,380 fewer deaths than pre-plan rates predicted (95% CI 324-4,435; about 1,190 per year on average). The study is ecological - confounders such as air pollution and heat-wave timing were not controlled - so this is an association with wide uncertainty, not proof the plan caused the decline. The examinable facts are the study's existence, period and estimate; the caveat belongs in the lesson.

Source: NRDC and partners, Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan 2019 easy-read (introduced 2013 after 2010 heat wave; Azhar et al. 2014: 1,344 additional deaths); Hess et al. 2018, J Environ Public Health, doi:10.1155/2018/7973519 (South Asia's first HAP, full implementation 2014; thresholds from local temperature-mortality epidemiology; est. 2,380 fewer deaths 2014-2015 vs pre-plan rates, 95% CI 324-4,435, ecological association)

Question 5

A city government studies its heat island using midday satellite thermal imagery (land surface temperature) together with night-time air-temperature readings from weather stations. Consider the following inferences drawn from this data:

  1. If the midday imagery shows dry farmland outside the city reading hotter than city surfaces, the city no longer has a heat island.
  2. Night-time readings showing warmer air downtown than outside the city indicate an atmospheric heat island, even if the midday surface-temperature contrast is small.
  3. The hottest rooftop clusters on the midday imagery are candidate sites for cool-roof or green-roof retrofits.
  4. Very hot rooftop readings on the midday imagery show that the air inside the homes below is equally hot.

Which of the above inferences are valid?

Correct answer: B

Inferences 2 and 3 only are valid. Inference 1 fails: dry, sparsely vegetated rural surfaces can heat faster than city surfaces in daytime - NASA's training material describes exactly this arid-land pattern - yet the same city can still end the night warmer, because its materials release stored heat slowly; a single midday surface map cannot retire a heat island. Inference 2 holds: warmer canopy-layer air downtown at night is precisely how an atmospheric heat island shows up, and it can coexist with a modest daytime surface contrast - surface and atmospheric heat islands are different quantities, so one small reading does not cancel the other. Inference 3 holds: locating the hottest surfaces from LST maps and targeting reflective or planted retrofits at them is the standard mitigation-targeting use of these maps. Inference 4 fails: a rooftop pixel measures the roof surface, a different quantity from indoor air; the air inside homes depends on ventilation, shading and construction, and a conventional roof can run dramatically hotter than the surrounding air while interiors stay far cooler. Option A wrongly validates inference 1; option C wrongly adds inference 4; option D wrongly validates inference 1.

Source: NASA ARSET UHI training Q&A, Part 1 (Q7 arid daytime exception, night-time release); US EPA, What Are Heat Islands and Compendium of Strategies (surface vs air day-night behaviour; hotspot targeting for retrofits)
