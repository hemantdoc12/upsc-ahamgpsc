What can a citizen actually ask the government?

Less to read. More to connect. Yesterday: who checks how public money is spent. Today, the citizen's own instrument: the Right to Information - what it reaches, what it never does, and the route from question to record, mapped to fifteen exam angles.

One law, one officer, four clocks, two appeals - and fifteen doorways into them: who holds the right, what counts as information, how to ask, the exemption shelf, the new 8(1)(j), the twenty-year sunset, the appeal map, and why the law reaches records, not answers. Each angle is a doorway, not a question.

The question today

Primary-source checked

The anchor: records, not compilations

Delhi High Court, Justice Amit Mahajan, order 29 October (reported 2 November): the court set aside the CIC's 26 May 2011 order that had directed the High Court's own CPIO to compile judge-wise lists of Justice A.K. Sikri's delayed judgments, sought in P.D. Gupta's 2009 request. The Right to Information reaches information available with the authority; no one can be made to collect, collate or create it - the court followed CBSE v Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011) and a Division Bench in Registrar SC v Commodore Lokesh Batra.

The route, one line per link

Ask in writing, no reason needed (s.6). The PIO's clocks run: thirty days, forty-eight hours for life and liberty (s.7). Exemptions are checked (ss.8-9), parts severed and third parties heard where needed (ss.10-11). Silence is a deemed refusal, appealable to an officer senior to the PIO, then to the Information Commission (s.19). The Commission can summon any record and penalise wrongful delay (ss.18(4), 20).

From the money chain to the citizen's ask

Yesterday's chain - approve, spend, account, audit, examine - runs inside Parliament. Today the citizen enters: the same records the PAC reads, section 3 lets any citizen request directly. The committees examine for Parliament; the RTI lets you examine for yourself. Information starts accountability; it never finishes it.

Four separate clocks

Section 7(1): the PIO must decide within thirty days. Life or liberty at stake: forty-eight hours. Filed through an Assistant PIO: add five days (s.5). A fee-intimation pause (s.7(3)) stops the clock until paid. Four clocks, never one.

Silence and lateness

No decision in time is a deemed refusal - the applicant may appeal as if refused (s.7(2) with 19(1)). Silence is never a deemed approval. And when the authority breaches its own time limit, the information comes free of charge (s.7(6)).

Section 8(3): ask about anything twenty years old and most exemptions fall away. Three survive the sunset: 8(1)(a) sovereignty and security, 8(1)(c) parliamentary privilege, 8(1)(i) cabinet papers. Time opens files - not all of them.

The appeal map

First appeal: an officer senior to the PIO, within thirty days (s.19(1)); disposal in thirty, at most forty-five with written reasons (19(6)). Second appeal: the Information Commission within ninety days (19(3)). The onus of justifying denial sits on the PIO (19(5)); the Commission's decision binds (19(7)).

Records, not answers

Today's MCQ: answer and why

Under the RTI Act, 2005 as in force today, which statement is correct?

Answer: (b)

(b) is right: since 13 November 2025, s.8(1)(j) reads simply "information which relates to personal information" (DPDP Act 2023, s.44(3); GSR 843(E)).

(a) fails: s.6(2) says no reason for requesting information may be required - only contact details and the particulars of what is sought.

(c) fails: a second appeal lies within 90 days (s.19(3)); 30 days is the filing window for the FIRST appeal (s.19(1)).

(d) fails: missing the time limit is a deemed refusal (appealable), and information that is due then comes free of charge (s.7(6)) - it is never deemed disclosed. Sources: RTI Act 2005, CIC consolidated text as on 18 Nov 2025.
