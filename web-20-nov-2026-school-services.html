School services and child rights · Five questions, fifteen connected angles

A school service is more than a category

Read school-service ladders, test what an indicator proves, and connect inclusion with children's rights.

Educational, hypothetical school cases. No local school statistic or compliance verdict is asserted. WHO/UNICEF monitoring, the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Indian RTE Act are distinct frameworks. A basic category is not a complete rights or accessibility audit.

Fifteen connections

01 / 15

Start with three school-service ladders

A school report needs separate judgements for drinking water, sanitation and hygiene. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme uses basic, limited and no-service categories for each. One successful component does not give the whole school a single basic-WASH certificate. These are school indicators, not the household sanitation ladder.

02 / 15

Identify the drinking-water source

For the school water ladder, first identify whether the drinking-water source is improved. Improved refers to a source type designed to protect against contamination; it is not a laboratory result for that particular water sample. A tap photograph alone does not answer the availability question.

03 / 15

Add water availability, with a time boundary

An improved drinking-water source with water available at school at the time of the survey meets the basic school-water criterion. An improved source without water available is limited service. An unimproved source, or no source, is no service. A finding on survey day does not establish uninterrupted supply throughout the year.

04 / 15

Do not turn a monitoring proxy into a safety certificate

The core school-water indicator uses source type and availability. Expanded questions separately examine treatment, testing against national standards, shortages, and accessibility. Thus a basic school-water classification does not by itself establish that all chemical and microbial requirements have been tested and met. This is a limit of the indicator, not a reason to ignore it.

05 / 15

Build the primary-school sanitation test

For a primary school, basic sanitation requires improved facilities that are single-sex and usable at the survey. Improved facilities that are not single-sex or not usable are limited service. Unimproved facilities, or no toilets, are no service. State the education level before applying an exception.

jmp methods

06 / 15

Test the missing condition, not the toilet label

For primary-school basic sanitation, three usability checks work together: availability to students, functionality and privacy. An always-available key can satisfy the availability check, but it cannot repair a blocked toilet or provide privacy. Water is necessary for flush or pour-flush functionality; do not turn that technology-specific condition into a claim that every dry toilet needs flushing water. A controlled comparison identifies which condition failed instead of merely counting buildings.

07 / 15

Keep the pre-primary exception narrow

Under current JMP methods, a pre-primary school with usable improved toilets may still be considered to have basic sanitation without single-sex provision. Apply this only within the permitted pre-primary exception, not to a primary-school case. The core-question guidance also notes age-appropriate qualifications, including that lockable doors may not apply in pre-primary settings. A monitoring exception is not a blanket legal exemption.

08 / 15

Read the hygiene ladder on its own terms

Handwashing facilities with water and soap available meet the basic school-hygiene criterion. Water but no soap means limited service. No handwashing facility, or no water for handwashing, means no service. A bottle of soap beside a dry facility therefore does not establish basic hygiene.

jmp methods · jmp core

09 / 15

Classify the cleansing material correctly

For JMP school monitoring, soap includes bar or liquid soap, powder detergent and soapy water. Ash, soil and sand do not count as soap. This is an indicator definition, not a claim that all materials have identical health effects or that monitoring categories measure every aspect of handwashing behaviour.

10 / 15

Separate availability from inclusive access

A facility may meet a core monitoring threshold while important access barriers remain. JMP expanded questions examine access for children with limited mobility or vision and for the smallest children. The broader SDG 4.a goal calls for child-, disability- and gender-sensitive education facilities. Do not read a basic category as proof of access for every child.

jmp core · sdg 4

11 / 15

Match the claim to the inspection

One visit can classify the conditions observed under the stated indicator. Claims about year-round continuity, equitable access or safe water need the corresponding evidence. A useful school audit links observations, water-quality evidence where relevant, maintenance records and user accounts without pretending that any one record proves all four. This audit bundle is a teaching synthesis, not a prescribed JMP form.

12 / 15

Place the Indian school standard beside the indicator

The RTE Act Schedule lists barrier-free access, separate toilets for boys and girls, and safe and adequate drinking water for all children among school-building norms. The Act concerns the right to free and compulsory elementary education, with its stated six-to-fourteen age framework and legal qualifications. A JMP category does not decide compliance with the Act or other applicable standards.

13 / 15

Connect health and education without merging the instruments

CRC Article 24 concerns health, including clean drinking water and education about hygiene and environmental sanitation. Article 28 concerns education and includes measures encouraging regular attendance and reducing drop-out rates. These rights help explain why school services matter; they do not supply the numerical cut-offs for a JMP service category or prove a local causal effect on attendance.

14 / 15

Treat child participation as evidence and a right

CRC Article 12 gives a child capable of forming views the right to express them freely in matters affecting the child, with due weight according to age and maturity. Listening to children can reveal barriers an inventory misses. This is neither a token consultation nor an automatic child veto over every school decision.

Use the observance as a question, not a certificate

World Children's Day is observed on 20 November. The date marks adoption of the 1959 Declaration of the Rights of the Child and the 1989 Convention on the Rights of the Child. The live UN page emphasises children's rights and voices. For this lesson, ask what the school-service evidence establishes, what a broader rights assessment must still examine, and whose experience is missing. No current school, district or Gujarat statistic is asserted.

Five Prelims questions

Questions 1-2 form Prelims 1/3, 3-4 form Prelims 2/3, and question 5 is HARD. Choose first, then examine every option.

Question 1 / 5

A primary school has an improved drinking-water source. Water is available at school during the survey, but water-quality test results are not available. Under the WHO/UNICEF school-service ladder, which conclusion is best supported?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. The core school-water category uses an improved source and availability at the survey; it does not automatically require a test result for the basic threshold.

B. Correct. Both stated core conditions are met. Testing against water-quality standards is a separate evidence question.

C. Wrong. The JMP explicitly uses improved source type in this monitoring indicator.

D. Wrong. The basic finding is supported, but the year-round continuity claim goes beyond the survey evidence.

Question 2 / 5

Two schools have improved toilets that are usable at the survey, but neither has separate facilities for boys and girls. One is exclusively pre-primary; the other is a primary school. Apply the permitted WHO/UNICEF pre-primary exception to the first school. Which school-sanitation classification follows?

Correct answer: D

A. Wrong. Primary-school basic sanitation requires single-sex provision as well as improved and usable facilities.

B. Wrong. The current methods explicitly allow the pre-primary exception when improved facilities are usable.

C. Wrong. This reverses the direction of the pre-primary exception.

D. Correct. The pre-primary exception applies to that school only; the primary school lacks one basic-service condition. This does not decide compliance with Indian law.

Question 3 / 5

A school has a handwashing facility with water and ash, but no soap. Under the WHO/UNICEF school-hygiene ladder, what is the service category?

Correct answer: A

A. Correct. Water but no qualifying soap means limited hygiene service; ash is not soap for this indicator.

B. Wrong. The JMP excludes ash, soil and sand from the soap category.

C. Wrong. No facility or no water would mean no service. Missing soap with water is limited.

D. Wrong. Basic hygiene also requires water and soap available at the facility.

Question 4 / 5

A school meets the core WHO/UNICEF service indicators. Children report that younger pupils cannot reach some taps. Which response best respects the limits of monitoring and the Convention on the Rights of the Child?

Correct answer: C

A. Wrong. The core category is not a complete rights or accessibility audit. Expanded monitoring explicitly examines access by small children.

B. Wrong. A concern deserves investigation, but classification still depends on the conditions of each indicator.

C. Correct. This keeps the monitoring claim within its scope and applies Article 12 without converting participation into an automatic veto.

D. Wrong. Article 12 requires due weight according to age and maturity, not an unconditional power to dictate every outcome.

Question 5 / 5 · HARD

At a primary school, visit one finds an improved drinking-water source with water available, usable single-sex pit latrines with slabs that do not require flushing water, and a handwashing facility with water and ash but no soap. At visit two, soap is supplied, but water is unavailable for drinking and handwashing. The toilets remain usable.
Consider these inferences under the WHO/UNICEF school-service ladders:
1. Every service category must decline at visit two.
2. Hygiene moves from limited service to no service despite the new soap.
3. Basic sanitation alone cannot settle whether children with disabilities can use the facilities.
4. Basic water service at visit one certifies compliance with all water-quality standards.
Which inferences are correct?

Correct answer: B

A. Wrong. Inference 2 is correct, but 1 is not: sanitation remains basic on the facts given, although water and hygiene categories decline.

B. Correct. Water without qualifying soap gives limited hygiene at visit one; no water gives no hygiene service at visit two. The basic sanitation indicator is not a complete disability-access assessment.

C. Wrong. Inference 2 is correct, but 4 mistakes a source-and-availability monitoring threshold for comprehensive water-quality certification.

D. Wrong. Inference 3 is correct, but 4 overstates the basic school-water finding. Water testing is a separate evidence question.
